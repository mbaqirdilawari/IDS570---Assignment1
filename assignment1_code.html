    “haue” (163 in Text A, 86 in Text B)
    “bee” (108 in Text A, 51 in Text B)
    “hath” (87 in Text A, 52 in Text B)
    All three words showed up in the “Top 15” list, once I stopped filtering the custom Early Modern words. These are exactly the words which “custom_stopwords” list was built to catch.

  • Why might these words be unhelpful if our goal is to identify the subject matter of the documents?

    These are just old-fashioned versions of basic grammatical verbs, words used constantly in almost any sentence, no matter what it’s about. Knowing a text uses “haue” a lot does not tell us whether it’s about a certain topic, for example, trade, farming, or religion.

  • Can you imagine a research question for which one of these words would be useful evidence?

    If the research question was about language and spelling itself rather than subject matter, these words could be useful. For example, “How did English spelling conventions for common verbs (like ‘have’ to ‘haue’, ‘hath’ to ‘has’) change over the 16th and 17th centuries?” Here, the frequency of “haue” or “hath” becomes the evidence I would want to study, rather than noise to remove.

  • Based on this experiment, explain why there is no single, universally “correct” stopword list.

    This experiment shows that whether a word counts as a “stopword” depends entirely on what question we are asking, and not on some fixed property of the word itself. For a study about trade and commerce, “haue” and “hath” are irrelevant noise worth removing.
    However, for a study about historical spelling or language change, those same exact words become the main subject of interest. Since different research questions need different words removed or kept, no single universal stopword list can work for every project. The list has to be built around the specific question being asked.

Final Reflection

For this specific analysis, the student is only partly right. The words we removed genuinely were unimportant for our research question, since more than half of each document’s tokens (56.6% for Text A, 59.8% for Text B) were filler words like “the,” “of,” “and” that added no insight into the subject matter, and removing them revealed meaningful content words like “trade” and “exchange” instead.
However, “unimportant” is not a fixed property of a word. It depends entirely on what you’re studying, and that’s exactly what our custom stopword experiment showed. When we chose to keep old English words like “haue,” “bee,” and “hath” instead of filtering them out, they appeared prominently in our results. This means that the same word can be irrelevant noise in one analysis and genuinely important evidence in another. For example, in a study about historical spelling patterns or language change. So the claim only holds for this particular text and this particular question. But for other types of text, such as poetry or legal documents, the same “unimportant” words might carry emphasis or patterns that are actually vital to what is being studied. Therefore, stopword removal is never just data cleaning, but a judgment call that has to be made differently for each new data set or research question.
